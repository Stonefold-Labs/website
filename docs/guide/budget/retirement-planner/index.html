Guide / Budget

The retirement planner

See how a plan holds up, year by year, with your own numbers.

How do I turn it on?

In Settings ▸ Features: Budget, turn on Retirement planning. It is off to start. The Mac’s budget sidebar then gains Retirement, after Reports, and the command box finds it and New Retirement Plan…. Each one is a “retirement plan”: “Plan” is already the budget’s monthly plan. Plans are kept in the budget’s file, encrypted. On the iPhone, Retirement is in the budget’s list of screens.

Starting a plan

New Retirement Plan… asks four things: the year you were born, the age you’d like to retire at, what you spend a year, and what you earn a year. It fills in the rest. Your budget’s own open accounts, not cards or loans, are read live, with an account in the budget as cash and a tracking account as taxable. You set each one’s type. To start it assumes an end age of 95, inflation 3%, tax 15%, returns of 6% and 2%, volatility 12% and a safe withdrawal rate of 4%. The plan’s page says what it assumed, each with Change beside it. On the Mac, click any part of the plan to change it, or + beside a list to add to it. On the iPhone, tap + to make a plan, tap any part of it to change it, or + beside a list to add to it.

The page

The answer comes first: whether your money lasts to 95 or runs out at an age, the chance of success, and the target and when it is met. Then a chart of net worth over your life, with retirement and milestones marked, and the plan’s parts in short lists: Accounts, Income, Spending, Saving, Milestones and Social Security. Each part is a sheet. On the Mac, By Year shows every year: age, income, Social Security, taxes, spending, saved, taken out and net worth, in today’s money or that year’s. On the iPhone, By Year shows every year’s net worth in today’s money. Change… opens More About This Plan: the ages, the rates, the withdrawal order, the leftover account, the safe rate and the number of tries.

Comparing plans

To compare, choose Duplicate from the plan’s menu, then change the copy: retiring at 60 and at 65, say. On the Mac, Compare draws up to three plans side by side.

What goes in a plan

  • Accounts: cash, taxable, tax-deferred (such as a 401(k) or traditional IRA), Roth and HSA. Each balance is read from one of your budget’s accounts, or typed, and grows at the plan’s rate for its type, or a rate of its own.
  • Streams: income, spending, and contributions into an account, by you or by an employer. Each is in today’s money and rises with prices unless you say not, from now, an age, retirement or a milestone, up to another.
  • Milestones: retiring, and any others at an age: a house, college, a windfall. Each can bring money once, and money yearly for some years.
  • Social Security: typed from your own statement: the monthly benefit at full retirement age (67 for anyone born in 1960 or later, or set yours) and the age you will claim it, 62 to 70. The claiming age changes the benefit by the Social Security Act’s rule for early and late claims. It rises with prices.
  • Taxes: one rate you type, on taxed income and Social Security less what is put in before tax, on all of a tax-deferred withdrawal, and on the growth share of a taxable one.
  • Withdrawals: when a year spends more than comes in, from the accounts in a plain order: cash, taxable, tax-deferred, Roth, HSA. You can change the order under More.

The target and the chance of success

The target is a year of retirement’s spending over a safe withdrawal rate, in today’s money, and the age at which your accounts first hold it. The chance of success is worked out from a thousand lives on your device, each drawing every year’s market return from a normal curve with the expected return as its middle and a volatility you set as its spread. Cash never moves with the markets. The same plan always gives the same chance. It is shown beside the plain projection, with the band one life in ten either side.

What it leaves out

It is an estimate, not financial advice. Today is taken as the start of this year. A year grows first, and its money comes and goes at its end. Growth isn’t taxed until it is taken out. No tax tables, early-withdrawal penalties, contribution limits or required withdrawals are counted, and the plan follows one person’s ages: a partner’s income is a stream from an age. Like everything Stonefold works out, it is plain arithmetic, with no AI.
